Question: at finite n, how singular does a model look, and how much of function space does the free energy actually see?

As in the lecture, n is a parameter scaling the population loss, not a sample count. It is related to generalization from n samples through Watanabe’s formula, and we ignore sample noise throughout.

What to do first. Two exercises carry the lecture: 5.2a (a cosine integral, and the truncation theorem it gives) and 5.3a (Newton polygons in one parameter). Do those, then pick from the rest.

  • 5.1: 5.1a is optional, for anyone who hasn’t seen why generic functions are non-singular. 5.1b is the two-plateau picture from the lecture (lower priority).
  • 5.2: 5.2a is the core. 5.2b (optional) treats other activations through their nearest complex pole. 5.2c–d are low priority: do §5.3 first.
  • 5.3: 5.3a is the core. 5.3b adds a second parameter and shows where counting by sizes fails; 5.3c is Furman’s cubic model at finite n.
  • 5.4: an optional sheet on tropical arithmetic, the algebra behind Newton polygons.

The learning-zero challenge that goes with the second lecture is in Notebook 6.

Notation. Inputs x\sim\gamma=\mathcal N(0,1); function space \mathcal F=L^2(\gamma) with \langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb E_{x\sim\gamma}[f(x)g(x)]. A model is a map \theta\mapsto f_\theta\in\mathcal F, \theta\in\mathbb R^d, with prior \varphi; the target is f^*\in\mathcal F, and

K(\theta)=\|f_\theta-f^*\|^2 .

With Gaussian output noise of variance \tfrac12 this is exactly the population excess negative log-likelihood; other noise levels rescale n. As in Notebook 2, Z_n=\int\varphi\,e^{-nK} and F_n=-\log Z_n; if K_*=\min K>0, the excess free energy is \bar F_n=F_n-nK_*. The effective exponent of Notebook 3 §3.1 (written \hat\lambda(n) in the lecture) is

\lambda_{\rm eff}(n)=\frac{d\bar F_n}{d\log n}=n\,\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[K-K_*]\;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\;\lambda .

The running example is the cosine network f_\theta(x)=\sum_{i=1}^Ha_i\cos(w_ix), \theta=(a,w)\in[-1,1]^{2H} with the uniform prior, and learning zero means f^*=0.

5.1 Generic models are regular

Question: are degenerate minima stable under small perturbations of the target or the activation?

Notation: \nabla K, \nabla^2K the gradient and Hessian in \theta. A critical point is non-degenerate if \nabla^2K is invertible there; K is Morse if all its critical points are non-degenerate. For the cosine network, c_w=\cos(w\,\cdot)\in\mathcal F.

Learning zero is singular for every activation: whenever a_i=0 the weight w_i is unconstrained, so the zero set of K contains whole coordinate planes. This is a coincidence of the target, not of the architecture. Exercise 5.1a shows that such coincidences disappear under almost every perturbation, so that with probability one the learning coefficient of a real model is d/2. Exercise 5.1b shows why this is less damning than it sounds: the perturbation sets a scale, and below it the singular model is what the free energy sees.

Exercise 5.1a (Tilting makes a loss Morse). Optional: for anyone who hasn’t seen why generic functions are non-singular. You may use Sard’s theorem: for a smooth map g:U\to\mathbb R^d on an open U\subset\mathbb R^d, the set of critical values \{g(\theta):\det dg(\theta)=0\} has Lebesgue measure zero.

  1. Let K be smooth on U and, for v\in\mathbb R^d, K_v(\theta)=K(\theta)-\langle v,\theta\rangle. Show that \theta is a degenerate critical point of K_v exactly when \nabla K(\theta)=v and \det\nabla^2K(\theta)=0. Conclude that K_v is Morse for almost every v.
  2. (General tilts.) Let G:U\to\mathbb R^m be smooth with dG(\theta) injective for every \theta, and K_v=K-\langle v,G\rangle, v\in\mathbb R^m. Show K_v is Morse for almost every v.
    HintNear \theta_0 choose d components G_S with dG_S(\theta_0) invertible and use y=G_S(\theta) as local coordinates; for fixed v_{S^c} part 1 applies in y. Non-degeneracy of a critical point does not depend on coordinates. Finish with Fubini and a countable cover.
  3. (Perturbing the target.) Replace f^* by f^*+\sum_{j=1}^mv_jg_j for fixed g_j\in\mathcal F. Show that K changes by a tilt with G_j(\theta)=2\langle f_\theta,g_j\rangle, plus a constant. Deduce: on a compact set where the tangent functions \partial f_\theta/\partial\theta_\alpha are linearly independent, almost every such perturbation makes all critical points non-degenerate, once m is large and the g_j are the first m elements of an orthonormal basis.
  4. (Dead neurons are not minima.) For the cosine network and any target, let \theta be a critical point with a_i=0, and r=f_\theta-f^*. Show that the (a_i,w_i) block of \nabla^2K is \begin{pmatrix}2\|c_{w_i}\|^2&2b_i\\2b_i&0\end{pmatrix} with b_i=\langle r,\partial_wc_w|_{w=w_i}\rangle. Conclude that such a point is a saddle or degenerate, and degenerate only if w\mapsto\langle r,c_w\rangle has a double zero at w_i.
    Hint\partial_{w_i}f_\theta=a_i\,\partial_wc_{w_i} vanishes at a_i=0.
Solution

1. \nabla K_v=\nabla K-v and \nabla^2K_v=\nabla^2K=d(\nabla K). A degenerate critical point of K_v is a point where \nabla K=v and d(\nabla K) is singular, so v is a critical value of \nabla K:U\to\mathbb R^d. By Sard these v form a null set.

2. On a neighbourhood U_0 of \theta_0 where y=G_S(\theta) is a diffeomorphism, K_v=\tilde K(y)-\langle v_S,y\rangle with \tilde K=K-\langle v_{S^c},G_{S^c}\rangle. For each fixed v_{S^c}, part 1 says K_v is Morse on U_0 for almost every v_S; by Fubini, for almost every v. Countably many such U_0 cover U, and a countable union of null sets is null.

3. \|f_\theta-f^*-\sum_jv_jg_j\|^2=K(\theta)-2\sum_jv_j\langle f_\theta,g_j\rangle+\big(2\sum_jv_j\langle f^*,g_j\rangle+\|\sum_jv_jg_j\|^2\big), and the bracket does not depend on \theta. Here dG has entries 2\langle\partial_\alpha f_\theta,g_j\rangle, injective iff the projections of the tangent functions to \mathrm{span}(g_j) are independent. If the \partial_\alpha f_\theta are independent on a compact C, the smallest eigenvalue of their Gram matrix is bounded below on C, and projecting onto the first m basis elements changes the Gram matrix by an amount tending to 0 uniformly on C; so for large m part 2 applies near C.

4. \partial_{a_i}K=2\langle r,c_{w_i}\rangle and \partial_{w_i}K=2a_i\langle r,\partial_wc_{w_i}\rangle. Differentiating again at a_i=0: \partial^2_{a_i}K=2\|c_{w_i}\|^2; \partial_{a_i}\partial_{w_i}K=2b_i (the other term carries a factor a_i); \partial^2_{w_i}K=2\|a_i\partial_wc_{w_i}\|^2+2a_i\langle r,\partial^2_wc_{w_i}\rangle=0. The block has determinant -4b_i^2. If b_i\ne0 it is indefinite and \theta is a saddle. If b_i=0, then together with \langle r,c_{w_i}\rangle=0 the function w\mapsto\langle r,c_w\rangle vanishes to second order at w_i, and the block is singular.

The remaining loci where the tangent functions are dependent (w_i=0, |w_i|=|w_j|) are handled the same way. Perturbing the activation instead of the target gives a tilt that depends nonlinearly on the perturbation; the conclusion is the same, with more bookkeeping.

Exercise 5.1b (A small target is learning zero until n\sim\varepsilon^{-2}). Lower priority: this is the two-plateau picture from the lecture.

  1. (Sandwich.) Fix any model and a target with \|f^*\|=\varepsilon, and write F^{(\varepsilon)}_n, F^{(0)}_n for the free energies with targets f^* and 0. Show that for n\varepsilon^2\le1 F^{(0)}_{n/2}-1\;\le\;F^{(\varepsilon)}_n\;\le\;F^{(0)}_{2n}+2 . Deduce that if F^{(0)}_n=\lambda_0\log n+O(1), then \bar F^{(\varepsilon)}_n=\lambda_0\log n+O(1) for all n\le\varepsilon^{-2}, with constants independent of \varepsilon.
    Hint\|f_\theta-f^*\| lies between \|f_\theta\|-\varepsilon and \|f_\theta\|+\varepsilon; use (x+y)^2\le2x^2+2y^2 and (x-y)^2\ge\tfrac12x^2-y^2.
  2. (One neuron.) Take f_\theta=a\,c_w with (a,w)\in[-1,1]^2, and target \varepsilon h with h=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(c_{w_0}/\|c_{w_0}\|+x\big), w_0=0.6. Show \langle c_w,c_v\rangle=\tfrac12\big(e^{-(w-v)^2/2}+e^{-(w+v)^2/2}\big), \langle c_w,x\rangle=0, and K(a,w)=\big(a\|c_w\|-\varepsilon\rho(w)\big)^2+\varepsilon^2\big(1-\rho(w)^2\big),\qquad\rho(w)=\frac{\langle c_w,h\rangle}{\|c_w\|}.
  3. Show \rho^2\le\tfrac12 with equality exactly at w=\pm w_0. So K_*=\varepsilon^2/2, and the Hessian at the minima has one eigenvalue of order 1 and one of order \varepsilon^2.
    HintCauchy–Schwarz for \langle c_w,c_{w_0}\rangle. For the Hessian use the coordinates (\alpha,w) with \alpha=a\|c_w\|-\varepsilon\rho(w): at a critical point the Hessian transforms by congruence.
  4. Learning zero with one neuron has \lambda_0=\tfrac12. Conclude that \lambda_{\rm eff}(n)\approx\tfrac12 for n\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2} and \lambda_{\rm eff}(n)\to1 for n\gg\varepsilon^{-2}. The code below checks this.
Solution

1. By the triangle inequality K_\varepsilon\le(\|f_\theta\|+\varepsilon)^2\le2K_0+2\varepsilon^2, so Z^{(\varepsilon)}_n\ge e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}Z^{(0)}_{2n}\ge e^{-2}Z^{(0)}_{2n}. Also K_\varepsilon\ge(\|f_\theta\|-\varepsilon)^2\ge\tfrac12K_0-\varepsilon^2, so Z^{(\varepsilon)}_n\le e^{n\varepsilon^2}Z^{(0)}_{n/2}\le e\,Z^{(0)}_{n/2}. Take logarithms. Finally 0\le nK_*\le n\varepsilon^2\le1, so \bar F and F differ by at most 1, and \lambda_0\log(2n), \lambda_0\log(n/2) differ from \lambda_0\log n by constants.

2. \cos(wx)\cos(vx)=\tfrac12[\cos((w-v)x)+\cos((w+v)x)] and \mathbb E\cos(sx)=e^{-s^2/2}; x\cos(wx) is odd. Then \|ac_w-\varepsilon h\|^2=a^2\|c_w\|^2-2a\varepsilon\langle c_w,h\rangle+\varepsilon^2; complete the square.

3. \langle c_w,h\rangle=\langle c_w,c_{w_0}\rangle/(\sqrt2\|c_{w_0}\|), so \rho^2=\tfrac12\cos^2\angle(c_w,c_{w_0})\le\tfrac12, with equality iff c_w=\pm c_{w_0}, i.e. w=\pm w_0. The first term of K can be made 0 for every w, so the minima are at a=\varepsilon\rho(w_0)/\|c_{w_0}\|, w=\pm w_0, with K_*=\varepsilon^2/2. In the coordinates (\alpha,w), K=\alpha^2+\varepsilon^2(1-\rho^2), with Hessian \mathrm{diag}\big(2,-\varepsilon^2(\rho^2)''(w_0)\big) at the minimum; (\rho^2)''(w_0)\approx-0.66. The change of coordinates has \partial\alpha/\partial a=\|c_w\|\asymp1, so the eigenvalues in (a,w) are of order 1 and \varepsilon^2.

4. For f^*=0, K_0=a^2\|c_w\|^2 with \|c_w\|^2\ge\tfrac12, so V(t)\asymp t^{1/2} and \lambda_0=\tfrac12. Part 1 gives \lambda_{\rm eff}\approx\tfrac12 up to n\approx\varepsilon^{-2}. For n\gg\varepsilon^{-2} the minimum is non-degenerate in two dimensions and \lambda=1. The crossover is where the posterior width (0.66\,n\varepsilon^2)^{-1/2} in w falls below the width of the prior around \pm w_0, so n\asymp\varepsilon^{-2} up to a constant.

Effective singularity. Exercise 5.1b is the general picture in miniature. The generic model is regular; the free energy of the singular model (here learning zero) governs it over the window n\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2}, where \varepsilon measures the distance from the singular situation. B = ip(w, w0) / np.sqrt(2 * ip(w0, w0))        # <c_w, h>
rho2 = B**2 / A


def logZ_small_target(n, eps):
    # log of (1/4) int_{[-1,1]^2} exp(-n K) da dw, with K = A a^2 - 2 eps B a + eps^2
    m, s = eps * B / A, np.sqrt(2 * n * A)    # in a: Gaussian with mean m, sd 1/s
    lo, hi = log_ndtr(s * (-1 - m)), log_ndtr(s * (1 - m))

    la = -n * eps**2 * (1 - rho2) + 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - np.log(s) + log_mass
    return logsumexp(la) + np.log(dw / 4)

5.2 Function space

Question: at sample size n, how many directions of function space does the free energy see?

Notation: \mathrm{He}_k is the monic polynomial of degree k that is orthogonal, in \mathcal F, to all polynomials of lower degree: \mathrm{He}_0=1, \mathrm{He}_1=x, \mathrm{He}_2=x^2-1, \mathrm{He}_3=x^3-3x. They satisfy \langle\mathrm{He}_j,\mathrm{He}_k\rangle=k!\,\delta_{jk}, so h_k=\mathrm{He}_k/\sqrt{k!} is an orthonormal basis. Write c_k(\theta)=\langle f_\theta,h_k\rangle and c_k^*=\langle f^*,h_k\rangle, so that K(\theta)=\sum_k(c_k(\theta)-c_k^*)^2. \mathcal F_{\le k}=\mathrm{span}(h_0,\dots,h_k) is the space of polynomials of degree \le k, and K_{\le k}=\sum_{j\le k}(c_j-c_j^*)^2 is the loss seen through it, with free energy F_{n,\le k} and posterior expectations \mathbb E^{\le k}_{\pi_n}.

You may use the identity \mathbb E_\gamma[f\,\mathrm{He}_k]=\mathbb E_\gamma[f^{(k)}] for smooth f whose derivatives grow at most polynomially. (It is k integrations by parts, using \mathrm{He}_k\,\gamma=(-1)^k\gamma^{(k)}.)

Exercise 5.2a (The cosine integral, and truncation). The core exercise of this section.

  2. Let f^*=0, or any polynomial of degree \le2k. Show that 0\le K-K_{\le2k}\le2H^2/(2k+2)! on the whole box.
    Hint|M_j|\le H, and \sum_{j>k}1/(2j)!\le2/(2k+2)!.
  3. (The truncation theorem.) Suppose (2k+2)!\ge2nH^2. Show that |F_n-F_{n,\le2k}|\le1, and that every observable A\ge0 satisfies e^{-1}\,\mathbb E^{\le2k}_{\pi_n}[A]\le\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[A]\le e\,\mathbb E^{\le2k}_{\pi_n}[A].
    HintWrite K=K_{\le2k}+R. Part 2 gives e^{-nR}\in[e^{-1},1]. Compare the partition functions, then the posterior densities.
  4. For H=100, find the smallest such k at n=10^6 and n=10^{12}. How does it grow with n?
Solution

1. By the identity, \langle\cos(w\,\cdot),\mathrm{He}_k\rangle=\mathbb E[\partial_x^k\cos(wx)]. For k=2j, \partial_x^{2j}\cos(wx)=(-1)^jw^{2j}\cos(wx), and \mathbb E\cos(wx)=e^{-w^2/2} (the characteristic function of the Gaussian). For odd k, \partial_x^k\cos(wx)=\pm w^k\sin(wx) is odd, so its mean is 0. Divide by \sqrt{k!} and sum over neurons with weights a_i.

2. The target has no components above degree 2k, so K-K_{\le2k}=\sum_{j>k}M_j^2/(2j)!. Since |a_ie^{-w_i^2/2}w_i^{2j}|\le1, |M_j|\le H, and \sum_{j>k}\frac1{(2j)!}\le\frac1{(2k+2)!}\sum_{m\ge0}(2k+3)^{-2m}\le\frac2{(2k+2)!}.

3. With R=K-K_{\le2k}\in[0,1/n], e^{-nR}\in[e^{-1},1]. So Z_n=\int\varphi\,e^{-nK_{\le2k}}e^{-nR} lies between e^{-1}Z_{n,\le2k} and Z_{n,\le2k}, which is the bound on F_n. The posterior densities satisfy \frac{\pi_n}{\pi^{\le2k}_n}=e^{-nR}\,\frac{Z_{n,\le2k}}{Z_n}\in[e^{-1},e], and integrating against A\ge0 gives the second claim. For \lambda_{\rm eff}=n\,\mathbb E_{\pi_n}[K] the same argument gives e^{-1}\lambda^{\le2k}_{\rm eff}\le\lambda_{\rm eff}\le e\,\lambda^{\le2k}_{\rm eff}+1.

4. The code below finds k=6 at n=10^6 (degree 12: the seven coordinates c_0,c_2,\dots,c_{12}) and k=9 at n=10^{12} (degree 18). By Stirling, (2k)!\approx nH^2 means 2k\log(2k)\approx\log n, so k grows like \log n/(2\log\log n), a little slower than \log n. At these values of n the asymptotic formula undershoots, because the lower-order terms in Stirling still matter.

1. All terms are nonnegative, so K is at least its largest term and at most J times it. Hence \{K<t\} contains \{\max_jc_ja^{2j}<t/J\}=\{|a|<\ell(t/J)\} and is contained in \{|a|<\ell(t)\}. With the uniform prior on [-1,1], the prior volume of \{|a|<r\} is \min(1,r).

4. F_n is, up to O(1), the upper envelope of the lines X\mapsto(X+\log c_j)/(2j) in X=\log n. Their slopes \tfrac1{2j} increase as j decreases, so as X grows the active j decreases. def lam_eff_cubic(n, mu0):
    # n E[K] under the posterior, K = (mu^3 - mu0^3)^2 / 2, mu uniform on [-1, 1]
    K = lambda u: 0.5 * (u**3 - mu0**3)**2
    w = 1 / (3 * mu0**2 * np.sqrt(n))                  # posterior width around mu0 once resolved; tell quad where to look
    pts = sorted({0.0, mu0} | {mu0 + c * w for c in (-10, -3, -1, 1, 3, 10) if -1 < mu0 + c * w < 1})

    E = quad(lambda u: n * K(u) * np.exp(-n * K(u)), -1, 1, **kw)[0]
    return E / Z

5.4 Optional: tropical arithmetic

Question: what algebra do exponents of n obey?

Notation: for a positive quantity A depending on n, its exponent is \log A/\log n. In this section \gamma denotes an exponent, not the Gaussian density.

§5.3 (and Notebook 6) compute free energies to O(1), so they only track exponents. This section makes the rules explicit. Exercise 5.4c is also the bookkeeping behind the staircases of Notebook 6, Exercise 6.1d.

Exercise 5.4a (Exponents add by taking the max).

  1. For x,y\in\mathbb R and n\ge2, show \max(x,y)\log n\;\le\;\log(n^x+n^y)\;\le\;\max(x,y)\log n+\log2 . So on exponents, sums become maxima and products become sums, with an error O(1) that is O(1/\log n) in units of \log n. The tropical semiring is \mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\} with x\oplus y=\max(x,y) and x\odot y=x+y. Check that \odot distributes over \oplus, that -\infty is the unit for \oplus, and that nothing has an inverse for \oplus.
  2. Show \log\sum_{j\le J}c_jn^{x_j}=\max_j\big(x_j\log n+\log c_j\big)+O(\log J) for c_j>0. A fixed constant c_j shifts the free energy by O(1) and never changes an exponent; it matters for exponents only when it is itself a power of n. Relate this to the phrase “coefficients comparable to 1/n”.
  3. (Two basins.) Notebook 2 Exercise 2.3 has F_n=-\log\big(e^{-F_n(U)}+e^{-F_n(V)}\big). Show |F_n-\min(F_n(U),F_n(V))|\le\log2: the free energy of a union is the tropical sum of the free energies, and a phase transition is a change of the active term.
Solution

1. n^{\max}\le n^x+n^y\le2n^{\max}. Distributivity: x+\max(y,z)=\max(x+y,x+z). \max(x,-\infty)=x. If \max(x,y)=-\infty then x=y=-\infty, so no finite x has an inverse: tropical algebra has no subtraction, which is why cancellation is invisible to it (Exercise 5.4d).

2. The sum lies between its largest term and J times it. Writing c_j=n^{\log c_j/\log n}, a constant changes an exponent by \log c_j/\log n\to0. In K=\varepsilon a^2+a^4 with fixed \varepsilon, the asymptotic exponent is \tfrac12 whatever \varepsilon is; with \varepsilon=n^{-\gamma} the coefficient enters the exponents and the regime depends on \gamma (Exercise 5.4b.3). “Comparable to 1/n” means: \log c is comparable to \log n.

3. \bar Z=e^{-F(U)}+e^{-F(V)} is between the larger term and twice it; take -\log.
